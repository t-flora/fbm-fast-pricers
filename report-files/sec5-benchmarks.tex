\section{Algorithm Benchmarks}
\label{sec:benchmarks}

All C++ benchmarks were run single-threaded on an Apple M2 (8-core, 24~GB unified
memory, rated DRAM bandwidth 100~GB/s; its largest cache is the 16~MB L2 shared by
the performance cores, and it has no L3), compiled with
\texttt{-O3 -march=native -DNDEBUG}, with $M=10{,}000$ paths and
$N \in \{252, 500, 1000\}$.  Timings come from a single benchmark run; back-to-back
repeats of the Cholesky pricer varied by up to $\approx 15\%$.  Reference prices use
$M=500{,}000$ paths (see §\ref{sec:errorrank}).

\subsection{Runtime scaling}
\label{sec:timing}

Measurement setup: controlled: $M = 10{,}000$ paths; independent: $N \in \{252,500,1000\}$
and method; dependent: total wall-clock time.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{time_vs_N.png}
  \caption{Wall-clock time vs.\ $N$ on log-log axes (left panel) with fitted power
    laws $t = c \cdot N^\alpha$, and fitted exponents $\alpha$ with prefactors $c$
    (right panel; dotted line = theoretical exponent).
    Controlled: $M=10{,}000$ paths.
    Independent: $N \in \{252, 500, 1000\}$, method.
    Dependent: total wall-clock time.}
  \label{fig:timing}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:timing} shows the results.  Log-log regression over three $N$ values
gives the fitted exponents in Table~\ref{tab:exponents}.

\begin{table}[t]
  \caption{Fitted power-law exponents $\alpha$ from log-log regression
    $\log t = \log c + \alpha \log N$ over $N \in \{252, 500, 1000\}$.
    Theoretical exponent assumes $O(MN^\alpha)$ total work.}
  \label{tab:exponents}
  \centering
  \begin{tabular}{lrrl}
    \toprule
    Method & Fitted $\alpha$ & Fitted $c$ & Theoretical $\alpha$ \\
    \midrule
    Dense Cholesky  & 1.33 & $1.1\times 10^{-4}$ & 2.0 \\
    Circulant + FFT & 0.95 & $1.5\times 10^{-3}$ & 1.0 \\
    Low-rank rSVD ($k=32$) & 1.02 & $3.6\times 10^{-4}$ & 1.0 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The Cholesky empirical exponent ($\alpha \approx 1.33$) is substantially below the
theoretical $2.0$.  This occurs because the per-path cost is not purely the $O(N^2)$
triangular multiply: drawing $N$ Gaussian variates, computing $N$ exponentials for the
stock price path, and accumulating the payoff each contribute $O(N)$ overhead that is
non-negligible for $N \leq 1000$.  As $N$ grows further, the $N^2$ term will
eventually dominate and the exponent will approach $2.0$; the tested range is still
in the crossover region.

The FFT and rSVD exponents are both $\approx 1.0$, consistent with theory.  The
$O(\log N)$ factor distinguishing $O(N\log N)$ from $O(N)$ is unresolvable over a
$4\times$ range in $N$ (a $\log$-ratio of $\log(1000)/\log(252) \approx 1.25$),
and appears as noise in the regression.

Absolute times matter as much as exponents.  At $N = 1000$ the three methods take
$1.13$~s (Cholesky), $1.04$~s (FFT), and $0.43$~s (rSVD, $k = 32$).  The FFT method
is only $\approx 9\%$ faster than Cholesky, even though its sampling step does an
order of magnitude less arithmetic per path ($\approx 10^5$ flops for a size-$2N$
complex FFT against $N^2 = 10^6$ for the triangular mat-vec).  Two effects explain
the gap.  First, the price-path work ($N$ Gaussian draws, $2N$ exponentials, and the
payoff average) is identical for all three methods and is a large share of every
per-path cost.  Second, the FFT sampler draws $4N$ Gaussian variates per path against
$N$ for Cholesky, and at $N = 1000$ those draws cost about eight times as much as the
inverse FFT itself (§\ref{sec:opt-opportunities}).  The asymptotic advantage is real,
and the gap widens with $N$ ($\alpha = 1.33$ against $0.95$), but at $N \leq 1000$
it is largely hidden by work the two methods share.  With the earlier dense-$L$
baseline (§\ref{sec:cholesky-impl}) the FFT method appeared $1.5\times$ faster, a
reminder that a speedup is only as meaningful as its baseline.

\subsection{Memory and cache pressure}
\label{sec:memory}

Controlled: $M = 10{,}000$ paths; Independent: $N$, method;
Dependent: size of the dominant arrays resident during the MC loop (C++), peak heap
allocation (Python via \texttt{tracemalloc}).  For C++ I report the analytical size
rather than measured RSS.  The benchmark's RSS change across a call is not a peak,
because buffers are freed before the call returns, and it can read zero.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{memory_vs_N.png}
  \caption{Size of the dominant array held during the MC loop vs.\ $N$ for all C++
    methods (left), and the effective rate at which the Cholesky loop streams $L$
    (right).  The dashed horizontal line marks the M2's 16~MB L2 cache; Cholesky and
    the rSVD variant that retains $C$ cross it at $N \approx 1450$.  The rSVD
    variant that frees $C$ before the MC loop stays far below it.
    Controlled: $M=10{,}000$ paths.
    Independent: $N$, method.
    Dependent: memory (MB) and effective streaming rate (GB/s), computed as bytes of
    $L$ read divided by wall time; this is not a DRAM measurement.}
  \label{fig:memory}
\end{figure}

At the tested sizes the Cholesky loop is not DRAM-bound.  At $N = 1000$, $L$
occupies $N^2 \times 8 = 7.6$~MB (the full matrix is allocated, though only its
lower half is read), which fits in the 16~MB L2.  Dividing the bytes of $L$ read in
a run by its wall time gives an effective streaming rate of $14$, $24$, and
$35$~GB/s at $N = 252$, $500$, and $1000$, well below the 100~GB/s DRAM rating.
Because $L$ is cache-resident, these figures measure how fast the loop consumes
$L$, not DRAM traffic.  Beyond $N \approx 1450$, $L$ no longer fits in L2 and every
path must stream $4N(N+1)$ bytes from DRAM, at which point the loop would become
bandwidth-limited \citep{drepper2007every}.

The rSVD method has two memory variants.  The standard variant retains $C$ in memory
during the MC loop (7.6~MB at $N = 1000$, the same footprint as Cholesky).  A
second variant, \texttt{price\_freed\_timed()}, releases $C$ immediately after
forming $L_k$; the resident footprint during the MC loop is then $O(Nk)$ (0.24~MB
at $N=1000$, $k=32$), far below the L2 capacity.  The FFT method needs only four
length-$2N$ complex buffers, 128~KB at $N = 1000$.

The Python RFSV engine (used in Sections~\ref{sec:validation}
and~\ref{sec:sensitivity}) is vectorized across paths and so has a different
profile.  \texttt{tracemalloc} on the log-volatility generator measures a peak of
$104$ bytes per (path, time step), i.e.\ 13 float64 words per element of the
$(M, N)$ output, at every $M \geq 1000$.  That count is consistent with the working
arrays the generator holds at once, which are $2N$ wide: two real Gaussian arrays
and two complex arrays of shape $(M, 2N)$ already account for 12 words per output
element.  At $M = 10{,}000$, $N = 252$ the peak is $\approx 260$~MB, far beyond any
cache.  The measurement covers path generation only; the price-path arrays come on
top.

\subsection{MC convergence}
\label{sec:convergence}

Controlled: $H=0.10$, $\nu=0.30$, $K=100$, $T=1$, $S_0=100$, $r=0$,
$N=252$; Independent: $M \in \{100, 250, 500, 1000, 2500, 5000,
10000, 25000\}$; Dependent: mean price and cross-seed standard deviation
over 5 independent random seeds.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{convergence.png}
  \caption{MC convergence: price $\pm 1\sigma$ vs.\ number of paths $M$ (left), and
    log-log plot of cross-seed standard deviation vs.\ $M$ with fitted slope (right).
    The shaded band in the left panel is $\pm 2\hat{\sigma}_\mathrm{payoff}/\sqrt{M}$
    around the C++ reference price, with $\hat{\sigma}_\mathrm{payoff}$ the sample
    standard deviation of the 25{,}000 individual payoffs of one run.
    Controlled: $N=252$, $H=0.10$, $\nu=0.30$.
    Independent: $M$ (left and right); 5 seeds (right).
    Dependent: mean price $\pm 1\sigma$ and log-log slope.}
  \label{fig:convergence}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} shows the results.  The fitted log-log slope is
$-0.711$ ($R^2 = 0.959$), steeper than the theoretical $-0.5$ from the central
limit theorem \citep{glasserman2004monte}.  The discrepancy arises from the small-$M$
regime: with only 5 seeds, the cross-seed standard deviation at $M \leq 500$ is a
noisy chi-squared estimate (4 degrees of freedom) with high variance, pulling
the regression slope away from $-0.5$.  (The sample standard deviation is in
fact a downward-biased estimator of $\sigma$ at finite sample sizes by Jensen's
inequality; the dominant effect here is its large variability, not a directional
bias.)  The theoretical
line at slope $-0.5$ is shown as a dotted reference in Figure~\ref{fig:convergence}(b);
the data approach it at large $M$ but deviate below $M \approx 1{,}000$.
The cross-seed standard deviation at small $M$ is also noisy because the payoff
itself is highly variable, with $\sigma_\mathrm{payoff} \approx 61$ (the sample
standard deviation over 200{,}000 paths).  An earlier version of this study
estimated $\sigma_\mathrm{payoff} \approx 35$ by rescaling the spread of only five
seed-level prices, an estimate with roughly $35\%$ relative error; the current code
uses the per-path payoffs directly.  The payoff is so variable because our RFSV
model uses
$\sigma_0 = e^{\nu \cdot 0} = 1.0$ (100\% annualised base volatility), producing
highly variable payoffs.  This is not a model defect but a consequence of using the
uncalibrated log-vol parameterisation $\log\sigma_t = \nu W_t^H$; the IV validation
in §\ref{sec:iv} auto-calibrates a drift $\mu_0 = \log\sigma_\mathrm{target}$ to
match market volatility levels.  A calibrated $\mu_0 \approx \log(0.18) \approx
-1.7$ would reduce $\sigma_\mathrm{payoff}$ by a factor of $\sim 5$ and the path
count required for the same precision by $\sim 25$; the uncalibrated parameterisation
was retained to keep the convergence study independent of the IV calibration procedure.

To achieve price uncertainty below 0.5 units (roughly 2\% of the ATM price
$\approx 23.6$) at 95\% confidence requires
$M \approx (2\sigma_\mathrm{payoff}/0.5)^2 \approx 60{,}000$ paths.  I use
$M = 10{,}000$ for most experiments, accepting $\approx \pm 1.2$ units per run
($2 \times \mathrm{SE}$, the 95\% confidence half-width).  Where differences between
runs matter, I use common random numbers or means over multiple seeds.  One caveat:
the reference line in Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} is the C++ reference at $N = 500$,
while this study simulates at $N = 252$; the averaging grids differ, so the line is a
visual guide rather than the exact target.

\subsection{Numerical stability}
\label{sec:stability}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{stability_report.png}
  \caption{Numerical stability of the three methods.
    \textbf{(a)} FFT: smallest circulant eigenvalue relative to $\gamma(0)$ vs.\ Hurst
    exponent $H$ ($N=252$ and $N=1008$); no eigenvalue is negative anywhere.
    \textbf{(b)} rSVD: condition number $\kappa(L_k)$ and Frobenius error vs.\ rank
    $k$ ($N=252$, $H=0.10$).
    \textbf{(c)} Cholesky: condition number $\kappa(C)$ vs.\ $N$ ($H=0.10$) with
    fitted power law.}
  \label{fig:stability}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{(a) FFT eigenvalue positivity: }
Figure~\ref{fig:stability}(a) sweeps $H \in [0.05, 0.501]$ at $N = 252$ and
$N = 1008$ and plots the smallest circulant eigenvalue relative to $\gamma(0)$.  No
eigenvalue is negative at any $H$ or $N$ tested, so nothing is clipped and the FFT
sampler is exact.  The minima agree with the closed form~\eqref{eq:lambda-min} to
every printed digit: $0.24\%$ at $H = 0.10$, $N = 252$ and $0.08\%$ at $N = 1000$,
always attained at $\lambda_0$.  The margin collapses as $H \to 0$, falling to
$0.07\%$ at $H = 0.05$, $N = 252$, but it is positive by construction and many orders
of magnitude above double-precision roundoff.  At $H = \nicefrac{1}{2}$ the
increments are white noise, every eigenvalue equals $\gamma(0)$, and the ratio is $1$.
Consistent with exactness, the 500{,}000-path FFT and Cholesky prices in
§\ref{sec:errorrank} agree to within MC noise.

\paragraph{(b) rSVD condition number: }
Figure~\ref{fig:stability}(b) plots $\kappa(L_k) = \sqrt{S_\mathrm{max}} /
\sqrt{S_\mathrm{min}}$ as a function of rank $k$ at $H = 0.10$, $N = 252$.  The
condition number grows from $8.2$ at $k = 8$ to $23.3$ at $k = 64$: as we include
smaller singular values (the denominator shrinks), $\kappa$ grows.  A condition number
of 23 is well within the range of float64 arithmetic ($\epsilon \approx 10^{-16}$,
so $\kappa \cdot \epsilon \approx 2 \times 10^{-15}$) and does not cause numerical
issues in path generation.

\paragraph{(c) Cholesky covariance conditioning: }
Figure~\ref{fig:stability}(c) shows $\kappa(C) = \lambda_\mathrm{max} /
\lambda_\mathrm{min}$ vs.\ $N$.  At $N = 252$, $\kappa(C) \approx 789$; at $N =
1000$, $\kappa(C) \approx 4 \times 10^3$.  The fitted power law is
$\kappa \approx 1.08\,N^{1.19}$: the covariance matrix becomes increasingly
ill-conditioned as more time steps are added, but only polynomially.  This growth is expected: adding time steps increases
correlations between adjacent points, pushing $\lambda_\mathrm{min}$ toward zero.
The matrix remains positive-definite and Cholesky-factorizable throughout the tested
range.  \texttt{Eigen::LLT} fails when floating-point roundoff destroys
positive-definiteness, which occurs when $\kappa(C) \cdot \epsilon_\mathrm{mach}
\geq 1$, i.e., $\kappa(C) \geq 10^{16}$.  At $N = 1000$ where
$\kappa(C) \approx 4 \times 10^3$, this threshold is twelve orders of magnitude
away; the fit would not reach it until $N \sim 10^{12}$.

The slow singular value decay visible in Figure~\ref{fig:structure}(d) is a
related but distinct phenomenon: a large $\kappa(C)$ means the spectrum spans
many decades, but slow decay (many non-negligible eigenvalues) is what
actually hinders low-rank approximation.  A matrix can have an enormous
condition number and still be perfectly approximated at low rank if the
eigenvalues drop off rapidly after the first few; in the fBM case, rough $H$
implies both a large condition number and a slow decay rate.  As
Figure~\ref{fig:structure}(d) shows, that slow decay belongs to the full matrix;
its well-separated off-diagonal blocks still compress to rank $\leq 3$.

\subsection{Low-rank approximation quality}
\label{sec:errorrank}

Controlled: $N = 500$, $M = 10{,}000$ paths (rSVD) and $M = 500{,}000$ paths
(reference); Independent: rSVD target rank $k \in \{2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64,
128\}$; Dependent: Frobenius error $\|C - C_k\|_F / \|C\|_F$ and
relative price error $|\hat{p}_k - p_\mathrm{ref}| / p_\mathrm{ref}$.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{error_vs_rank.png}
  \caption{Low-rank rSVD approximation quality vs.\ rank $k$.  Both the Frobenius
    error (noise-free, exact) and the price error relative to a 500{,}000-path
    reference are shown on shared log-scale axes.  The dashed horizontal line marks
    the MC noise floor at $M = 10{,}000$ paths.
    Controlled: $N=500$, $H=0.10$.
    Independent: rank $k$.
    Dependent: Frobenius error and relative price error.}
  \label{fig:errorrank}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:errorrank} shows results.  The reference price
$p_\mathrm{ref} = 23.58$ is the average of a 500{,}000-path Cholesky run ($23.62$)
and a 500{,}000-path FFT run ($23.54$); averaging two independent exact methods
avoids privileging either as ground truth.  With $\sigma_\mathrm{payoff} \approx 61$,
each run has a standard error of $\approx 0.09$.  Their difference of $0.08$ is below
the $0.12$ standard error of a difference, consistent with both methods being exact,
and the pooled reference has a standard error of $\approx 0.06$.

Two accuracy metrics are reported because they measure different things.  The
Frobenius error $\|C - C_k\|_F / \|C\|_F$ measures matrix approximation quality
exactly and without noise.  It decreases monotonically but slowly: $8.5\%$ at
$k = 2$, $3.5\%$ at $k = 8$, $1.9\%$ at $k = 32$, and $1.2\%$ at $k = 128$.  The price
error measures end-to-end accuracy but carries MC noise of $\approx 0.6$ units at
$M = 10{,}000$, i.e.\ $\approx 2.6\%$ of $p_\mathrm{ref}$.

The key limitation of this experiment is that the price errors are not resolved.
The observed relative errors range from $0.2\%$ ($k = 4$) to $3.1\%$ ($k = 2$), and
every one lies within $\approx 1.2$ standard errors of zero, so they do not
distinguish one rank from another.  The non-monotone pattern ($2.6\%$ at $k = 64$
against $0.2\%$ at $k = 4$) is noise.  Resolving a price bias of $0.1$ units would
need on the order of $M = 10^6$ paths per rank.  The Frobenius error is therefore the
meaningful accuracy metric here.  Its slow decay matches
Figure~\ref{fig:structure}(d): the spectrum of the full matrix $C$ decays only
algebraically, because the rough kernel's singularity lies on the diagonal, where a
global rank-$k$ approximation cannot isolate it.
