\section{Model Validation}
\label{sec:validation}

Three experiments probe the RFSV pricer: (i) the implied volatility smile
against live SPY option chain data; (ii) a comparison with the Lévy (1992)
analytical approximation and the roughness premium; and (iii) a parameter
sensitivity sweep over $H$ and $\nu$.  Each uses the Python RFSV engine
(\texttt{data/rfsv\_model.py}), which implements the circulant-FFT path
generator with the same normalization as the C++ FFT pricer.

\subsection{Implied volatility smile}
\label{sec:iv}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{validate_iv.png}
  \caption{RFSV vs market implied volatility smile for SPY, for the two nearest
    expirations in a snapshot taken on October 1, 2026 (spot \$763.99).
    Scatter: market IV computed by Black--Scholes inversion of mid-quote
    (\texttt{brentq}, $r = 0$, filtered to strikes in $[0.60S, 1.40S]$ with
    valid bid/ask spread); the $y$-axis is capped at $40\%$, and the 38 deep-ITM
    market IVs above it are marked as off scale.
    Line: RFSV IV, same inversion applied to Monte Carlo European call prices,
    with common random numbers across strikes and the sample mean of $S_T$
    matched to the forward.
    The RFSV log-vol drift $\mu_0$ is calibrated per expiration to match the
    ATM price (not IV): Brent's method on the price residual
    $p_\mathrm{RFSV} - p_\mathrm{mkt}(\sigma_\mathrm{ATM})$.
    The shaded region marks deep-ITM strikes where no simulated path finishes
    below $K$, so the Monte Carlo time value is zero and the IV is undefined.
    Controlled: $H = 0.10$, $\nu = 0.30$, $r = 0$, $M = 20{,}000$,
    $N = 63$ steps per year (at least 5 steps).
    Independent: strike $K$, expiration $T$.
    Dependent: implied volatility $\sigma_\mathrm{IV}$.}
  \label{fig:iv}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:iv} shows the results.  Because the uncalibrated RFSV model
sets $\sigma_0 = e^{\nu \cdot 0} = 1.0$ (100\% annualised vol) while SPY
trades near $\sigma \approx 14\%$ at the money, a direct level comparison is
not meaningful.  I instead add a log-vol drift: $\log\sigma_t = \mu_0 + \nu
W_t^H$, calibrating $\mu_0 = \log\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$ per expiration to match
the RFSV ATM price to the market ATM price.  This aligns the curves at the
money ($13.9\%$ and $14.5\%$ for the two expirations) so the comparison
concerns smile shape (slope and curvature), not the vol level.

The comparison is informative mainly about what the model, as implemented,
cannot do.  The RFSV smile is shallow and has its minimum at the money: for the
nearer expiration it rises from $13.9\%$ at the money to $\approx 14.3\%$ at
$K/S = 0.965$ and $\approx 14.7\%$ at $K/S = 1.05$.  The SPY smile is strongly
skewed instead.  It falls from $21.7\%$ at $K/S = 0.965$ to a minimum of
$\approx 10.7\%$ near $K/S = 1.03$ and recovers only to $13.4\%$ at
$K/S = 1.05$; the later expiration shows the same shape.  This is expected.  The
engine draws the price shocks independently of the volatility driver, so the
spot-vol correlation is $\rho = 0$, and with $\rho = 0$ a stochastic volatility
model produces a smile that is symmetric in log-moneyness.  It can generate
curvature but not skew.  The steep short-dated skew that rough volatility is
known for \citep{gatheral2018volatility} requires $\rho < 0$
(§\ref{sec:rho-future}).

\paragraph{A Monte Carlo pitfall: forward error masquerading as skew.}
An earlier run of this experiment used $M = 3{,}000$ paths with common random
numbers but no correction, and its RFSV curve rose steadily with strike, from
$13.0\%$ to $15.7\%$ across $0.98 \leq K/S \leq 1.05$.  That curve looks like an
upward skew, which a $\rho = 0$ model cannot produce.  The cause was sampling
error in the forward.  The sample mean of $S_T$ was $0.048\%$ below $S_0$, and
because every strike shares the same paths, that error acts like a shifted
forward and tilts the entire IV curve; it also biases the calibrated $\mu_0$.  A
400{,}000-path run of the same model gives an approximately symmetric smile with its minimum at
the money ($14.57\%$ at $K/S = 1.00$, against $14.79\%$ at $0.98$ and $14.70\%$
at $1.02$).  The current script rescales the simulated $S_T$ so that its sample
mean equals the forward exactly, a standard moment-matching correction
\citep{glasserman2004monte}, and uses $M = 20{,}000$ paths.  At $N \approx 5$
time steps per path the whole experiment runs in a few seconds.

\paragraph{Limitations}
I set $r = 0$ and ignore SPY's dividend yield.  For near-the-money options this
shifts IV levels slightly and leaves the shape comparison largely unaffected.
For deep in-the-money calls the effect is large: the carry in an ITM call price
is misread as time value, which inflates those market IVs (many above $40\%$),
so I do not interpret the low-strike wing.  SPY options are American-style; for
calls on a low-dividend ETF the early-exercise premium is small, and I treat
them as European.  The comparison is a single-day snapshot; market IV smiles
vary substantially day-to-day.  Brent's method stops at a tolerance of $0.01$ in
$\mu_0$, about $1\%$ in $\sigma_\mathrm{eff}$.

\subsection{Lévy benchmark and roughness premium}
\label{sec:levy}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{validate_asian.png}
  \caption{Asian option prices vs strike.
    \textbf{(a)} Price vs $K$: Lévy (1992) analytical approximation at
    $\sigma = 1.0$ (dashed, constant vol) and RFSV at four $H$ values
    ($\nu = 0.30$); error bars are $\pm 1\sigma$ over 3 independent seeds, with
    the same seeds used for every $H$.
    \textbf{(b)} Roughness premium $\hat{p}(H{=}0.10) - \hat{p}(H{=}0.50)$
    vs $K$, in option price units; peak near the money.
    Controlled: $S_0 = 100$, $T = 1$, $\nu = 0.30$, $r = 0$,
    $N = 252$, $M = 10{,}000$.
    Independent: $K$ (both panels); $H$ (left panel).
    Dependent: arithmetic Asian call price.}
  \label{fig:levy}
\end{figure}

Figure~\ref{fig:levy} shows the results.

\paragraph{Lévy is a weak benchmark at 100\% volatility: }
When $\nu \to 0$, $\sigma_t = e^{\nu W_t^H} \to 1$ pointwise, so the RFSV dynamics
reduce to constant-volatility GBM with $\sigma = 1$, and the Lévy approximation is
the natural closed-form comparison \citep{levy1992pricing}.  The approximation is
designed for typical equity volatilities, however, and at $\sigma = 1$ it is visibly
biased.  At $K = 100$ it gives $23.72$, while exact discrete GBM by Monte Carlo
(200{,}000 paths) gives $22.29 \pm 0.12$: Lévy overprices by $\approx 6\%$.  This
explains panel~(a), where the dashed Lévy curve lies on or above all four RFSV curves
at $K \leq 100$ and at $K = 120$, even though stochastic volatility should raise prices
above GBM.  The Lévy curve therefore cannot serve as a sanity check on the engine;
a $\nu = 0$ Monte Carlo run is the correct GBM baseline.  Against that baseline the
direction is as expected: the RFSV reference price $p_\mathrm{ref} = 23.58$
(§\ref{sec:errorrank}) is $\approx 1.3$ units above the GBM price, though on a
different time grid ($N = 500$ versus $N = 252$).

\paragraph{Roughness premium: }
The roughness premium in panel~(b) is $\hat{p}(H{=}0.10) -
\hat{p}(H{=}0.50)$, both at $\nu = 0.30$ and $\mu_0 = 0$ (uncalibrated,
$\sigma_0 = 1.0$), averaged over 3 seeds of $M = 10{,}000$ paths.  The premium is
positive at all nine strikes, between $0.42$ and $0.91$ price units, with the
largest value at $K = 105$.  The ATM value of $0.55$ is consistent with the
sensitivity sweep of §\ref{sec:sensitivity}, which gives $0.44$ at the same
$(H, \nu)$.

\paragraph{Limitations: }
The roughness premium as defined here conflates roughness with total variance.  At
fixed $\nu$, $\text{Var}(\log\sigma_t) = \nu^2 t^{2H}$ is larger for small $H$ when
$t < 1$, and the expected integrated variance
$\mathbb{E}[\int_0^T\sigma_t^2\,dt] = \int_0^T e^{2\nu^2 t^{2H}}\,dt$ is $\approx 1.16$
at $H = 0.10$ against $\approx 1.10$ at $H = 0.50$.  A $6\%$ increase in variance
raises an at-the-money price by roughly $3\%$, since the price scales approximately
with volatility; here that is $\approx 0.7$ units, comparable to the entire measured
premium.  The experiment therefore does not separate roughness from variance.  A
variance-normalised comparison, adjusting $\nu(H) = \nu_0\sqrt{\tfrac{2H+1}{2}\,T^{1-2H}}$
so that $\int_0^T \text{Var}(\log\sigma_t)\,dt$ is the same for every $H$, would
isolate the roughness effect; it is proposed in §\ref{sec:variancenorm}.

On noise: each price in panel~(a) is a 3-seed mean with a standard error of
$\approx 61/\sqrt{30{,}000} \approx 0.35$.  The premium is a difference of two such
means, but because the same seeds are used for every $H$ the two share their
random numbers, and the difference is far less noisy than the $\approx 0.5$ that
independent estimates would give.  I did not measure its standard error directly;
its sign is consistent across all nine strikes.

\subsection{Parameter sensitivity: }
\label{sec:sensitivity}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{sensitivity_surface.png}
  \caption{ATM Asian call price vs model parameters.
    \textbf{(a)} Heatmap: price vs $(H, \nu)$ at $K = S_0 = 100$ (viridis
    colormap; low H = rough = lower on the $y$-axis because the axis is
    inverted so H increases upward).
    \textbf{(b)} Price vs $H$ for each $\nu$; curves decrease left-to-right
    and separate vertically with $\nu$.
    All cells share the same random numbers, so differences between cells are
    precise even though each absolute price has an MC standard error of
    $\approx 0.6$.

    $M = 10{,}000$.
    Independent: $H$, $\nu$.
    Dependent: ATM Asian call price.}
  \label{fig:sensitivity}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{sensitivity_strike.png}
  \caption{Asian call price vs strike $K$ for varying $H$ at $\nu = 0.30$, with
    common random numbers across all curves.  Curves decrease monotonically with
    strike and are ordered by $H$ at every strike; the spread between $H = 0.05$
    and $H = 0.50$ widens from $\approx 0.3$ at $K = 80$ to $\approx 0.6$ at
    $K = 120$.
    Controlled: $\nu = 0.30$, $S_0 = 100$, $T = 1$, $r = 0$,

    Independent: $K$, $H$.

  \label{fig:sensitivitystrike}
\end{figure}

Figures~\ref{fig:sensitivity} and~\ref{fig:sensitivitystrike} show the
results:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Price decreases with $H$:} at $\nu = 0.30$ the ATM price falls
        from $23.60$ at $H = 0.10$ to $23.17$ at $H = 0.50$, and the effect grows
        with $\nu$ ($0.80$ units at $\nu = 0.40$, under $0.03$ at $\nu = 0.10$).
        Lower $H$ at fixed $\nu$ raises $\text{Var}(\log\sigma_t)$ as well as
        roughness (§\ref{sec:levy}), and a more variable $\sigma_t$ widens the
        distribution of the average $A$, which raises the expected value of the
        convex payoff $\max(A - K, 0)$.  The absolute spread across $H$ widens
        toward out-of-the-money strikes (Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivitystrike}).

  \item \textbf{Price increases with $\nu$:} at $H = 0.10$ the ATM price rises
        from $22.74$ at $\nu = 0.10$ to $24.31$ at $\nu = 0.40$.  Larger
        vol-of-vol amplifies $\sigma_t$ variation, and the increase is convex in
        $\nu$ because $\sigma_t = e^{\nu W_t^H}$ enters exponentially.

  \item \textbf{Price decreases with strike $K$:} standard convexity of the
        call payoff.
\end{itemize}

At the literature parameters $H = 0.10$, $\nu = 0.30$ with the uncalibrated base
volatility $\sigma_0 = e^{\nu \cdot 0} = 1.0$ (100\% annualised), the ATM price is
$23.60$, consistent with the reference price $p_\mathrm{ref} = 23.58$ from
§\ref{sec:errorrank} to well within MC error.  The 100\% base vol level is not
calibrated to market prices here (see §\ref{sec:iv} for that step).  Moving to the
Brownian limit $H = 0.50$ at the same $\nu$ gives $23.17$, a premium of $0.44$ price
units ($\approx 1.9\%$).  An earlier draft of this report quoted a premium of $2.1$
units; that run used an independent seed for every cell, and its cell-to-cell
differences were dominated by MC noise.

\paragraph{Limitations: }
All grid points share one random seed.  This makes the trends across the grid
reliable, but the whole surface carries a common MC offset with a standard error of
$\approx 0.6$ price units, so absolute prices are uncertain at that level.  The
sensitivity surfaces are computed on a $6 \times 4$ grid ($H \times \nu$) at a
fixed $N = 252$; finer grids and higher $M$ are feasible with the C++ benchmark but
were not run for this report.
